# Soundcheck

**Build the argument. Make every title say its part. Read the whole thing aloud.**

**Seven principles for humans and AI.**

Use Soundcheck by default when creating presentations or reviewing their titles,
and when asked to “run this deck through Soundcheck.” It governs the argument
and titles; the brief, audience, presenter voice and design system still govern
their own parts of the work.

These are principles, applied **as far as possible**. They are ordered by
priority: hold 1 and 2 most strongly, use 3–5 as strong defaults, aim for 6, and
reach for 7. A useful, coherent deck matters more than mechanical compliance.

## The one rule

> Don't use the title to describe what is on the slide. Use it to state the point the slide is making. The slide underneath exists to demonstrate, explain or prove that statement.

## The governing thought

A governing thought is the single claim that everything beneath it supports.
A slide's title is its governing thought. A deck has one too: its slide claims
should contribute to the same argument. Principles 1 and 7 operate at those
two scales.

## Working order: structure, then polish

First establish the audience and what the deck should help them understand,
believe or decide. Use the context already provided; ask only for missing
information that would materially change the argument. Agree the working mode
when it is open: full-deck review, conversational slide review, story test,
title spine before building, or diagnosis only when requested.

**Phase A — find the structure.** Start with principle 7. Draft the title spine:
the sequence of claims that forms the argument. Apply 1, 2 and 3 roughly while
doing so, then build the slides that demonstrate each claim. For an existing
deck, read its material and notes before deciding what argument is there.

**Phase B — sharpen the lines.** Once the material is in place, focus on 1 and 2,
then 3, 4 and 5, and trim with 6 last. Finally read the titles together again.
This is a two-phase process, not a literal countdown through the numbered list.

Two safeguards keep the structure honest:

- Identify the support or knowledge basis for each proposed spine claim, or
  identify the gap. An unsupported but plausible line is still provisional.
- Once the material is assembled, revisit the story. Change the argument when
  the evidence calls for it; polishing individual sentences cannot repair the
  wrong governing thought.

## Evidence can come from several places

Use **the AI's own world knowledge, research, or material supplied by the user**.
Evidence need not already appear on the slide. Bring in useful knowledge and
research to complete the argument, subject to the workspace's access and
confidentiality boundaries.

Distinguish established knowledge, sourced findings, inference and missing
support where that distinction affects the claim. Verify uncertain, changing
or consequential factual details and provide suitable sources. Stable general
knowledge can support a line without a citation on every slide. Never present
an inference as a verified result or invent facts to strengthen a sentence.

If a stronger title depends on new support, identify it and recommend where
the supporting material should go. For a title-only assignment, propose that
body or notes addition separately. Place citations according to the deck's
design and delivery mode; the reasoning must remain traceable.

The worked examples below illustrate the writing moves. Apply them only when
the relevant facts or reasoning support them in the actual assignment.

## 1 · Make a claim, not a label

*Hold this one.* A label names the subject. A claim tells us something about it.
The title states the point the slide underneath is there to demonstrate.

**Ask:** if a reader saw only this line, what would they now know? If the answer
is only which subject the slide covers, the point is still missing.

**Also ask:** does this title survive the audience looking at the slide?
“Activate with LinkedIn” adds little above LinkedIn screenshots. Explain the
choice or significance. A chart that visibly demonstrates the title's claim is
doing its job: repeating the takeaway in the title is assertion and evidence
working together, not a reason to invent a different claim.

## 2 · Make the claim carry the why

*Hold this one.* A true fact can still leave the audience wondering why it
matters. Move from a claim to the point you need this audience to understand.

**Ask:** **so what — to the person in the room?** Why does this point matter to
their decision or understanding, and does the title make that significance
clear? For a reading deck, ask the same question about the intended reader.

**Stop when the significance is clear to that audience for the job of this
slide.** The title need not explain the whole business case again. The body can
develop the reasoning and evidence; further “why?” questions belong there or
in a later slide if they serve a different point.

An illustrative ladder:

| Title | What it does |
|---|---|
| Q3 Performance | Names the subject |
| Q3 revenue grew 22% | States a fact |
| Q3 grew 22%, and almost all of it came from one channel | Identifies the concentration behind the result |

For a team deciding whether its growth is repeatable, that concentration gives
the fact significance. Another audience may need a different implication.

## 3 · One slide, one point — as far as possible

*Strong default.* Aim for one governing takeaway per slide, carried by its
title. Count ideas and relationships, not clauses, sentences or conjunctions.
The word “and” is not a litmus test.

**Ask:** what is the one thing the audience should take away? Do the parts of
this title combine to establish that takeaway, or does each ask the audience
to remember something independently?

A cause and effect, contrast, comparison, trade-off or before-and-after can
need two clauses to make **one** point. If separate claims compete for attention
and each needs its own conclusion, consider splitting the slide, choosing the
dominant point or making the supporting hierarchy clearer.

- **One relationship:** “Q3 grew 22%, and almost all of it came from one
  channel.” The concentration is the point. Splitting the clauses would weaken it.
- **One relationship:** “Make the title the point. Use the slide to prove it.”
  The two sentences explain how title and evidence work together.
- **Possibly two independent points:** “Search is cheap and LinkedIn reaches
  Deciders.” If this is a comparison proving how the channels complement each
  other, title that relationship. If it is two separate channel arguments,
  give each room of its own. Context decides.

## 4 · Write for the ear

*Strong default.* Write something a person could say to a colleague. Read it
aloud. Budget-line language, folder names and stock noun phrases usually need
rewriting. A finite verb often helps; fragments can earn their place through
rhythm and meaning.

**Ask:** would the presenter actually say this out loud?

“Heavy media spend on LinkedIn to reach key demographic of Deciders” can become
“Put the campaign in front of the people choosing suppliers,” when that is the
audience and purpose the plan supports.

Writing for the ear is deliberate. Keep the sound of a person speaking even
when the deck will also be read. Adapt to the requested voice and medium.

## 5 · Make the case fully

*Strong default.* Be specific about the conclusion you want the audience to
reach, and make the reasoning that earns it clear. Follow the argument through.
Anticipate the obvious “why this?”, “why not the alternative?” or “what about…?”
and resolve the gap that matters, drawing on the available knowledge, research
and supplied material.

The title gives the conclusion and decisive reason. The slide supplies the
evidence, explanation or comparison that completes the case. Keep any
qualification that materially changes the claim; supporting detail can live
underneath. One title still carries one point.

**Ask:** where could the audience reasonably say “hang on” because we skipped
a step, assumed something or left the obvious alternative unanswered? Make
that part of the case explicit. A useful claim does not need to provoke
disagreement, and undisputed does not mean uninformative.

For example, if the plan's targeting rationale supports it, “Activate with
LinkedIn” can become “Prioritise LinkedIn to reach the people choosing
suppliers.” The slide then shows who those people are, how the targeting
reaches them, and why this channel is the priority in this plan.

## 6 · Say it in as few words as possible

*Aim for it.* Short and punchy wins. Cut words that do not carry the point,
then stop. **Soundcheck has no word or character count.** A longer title that
lands the argument is worth more than an elegant line that loses it.

**Ask:** which words can go without losing the point, its necessary scope or
its spoken rhythm?

Template word, character and line limits are **layout budgets**. Flag a
collision and offer a viable shorter title or a layout adjustment while
preserving the point and its necessary scope.

## 7 · Read end to end, the titles tell one story

*The ideal — and the starting point in Phase A.* Read only the titles from
beginning to end. They should form a coherent argument, almost a continuous
paragraph. This is the Title Test: a diagnostic for thinking.

**Ask:** how does each slide's claim move the audience toward the deck's
governing thought? If the titles do not connect, look for a missing bridge, an
unnecessary detour, competing arguments, a missing why, or a point not yet found.

Find and repair the story before decorating disconnected claims with smoother
transitions. Respect the deliberate jobs of covers, dividers, reference pages
and rhetorical setups when reading the sequence.

## Where the principles relax

Plain labels can be the right title for covers and dividers, agendas, genuine
laundry lists, reference appendices, backup and methodology. A deliberate
one-word setup or rhetorical close can earn its effect through the surrounding
slides. “Summary” and “Next steps” are deprioritised, not banned. Ask whether
there is a useful point to state; if the slide's job is completeness or
navigation, name it honestly.

## How to help the writer

- **Give useful improvements, not scores.** Name the issue and offer a strong
  line, except when the user explicitly wants diagnosis only. Avoid pass/fail
  verdicts or a mechanical compliance checklist.
- **Offer strong alternatives when choice helps.** A small set can vary the
  emphasis, directness, rhythm or tone. Every option should be worth using;
  explain a meaningful trade-off and recommend one when useful. There is no
  fixed “better/best” format, and no reason to manufacture a weaker option.
  One clear improvement is enough when further alternatives add no value.
- **Keep good titles.** Say why briefly. If a weak-looking title is retained
  for its function, name the exemption rather than passing over it silently.
- **Respect the requested editing scope.** In a title review, propose body,
  evidence, slide-order or layout changes separately unless those edits are
  already authorised. If the argument has no point, consider cutting or
  merging instead of manufacturing a claim.
- **Check the audience and the story again at the end.** Confirm that the
  titles still mean what the supporting material establishes and serve the
  intended audience. Do not claim layout fit or factual verification that has
  not been performed.
